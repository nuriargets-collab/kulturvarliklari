The Hittites and the Neo-Hittite kingdoms

Civilizations and periods › The Hittites and the Neo-Hittite kingdoms

Stretching from central Anatolia to Syria, the Hittite kingdom was one of the great powers of the ancient Near East.

From the 17th century BC the Hittites built a powerful kingdom in central Anatolia, with its capital at Hattusa, in today's Boğazkale district of Çorum. Hittite, written in cuneiform on clay tablets, is one of the oldest known Indo-European languages. Hattusa's Lion Gate, King's Gate and Great Temple, together with the nearby open-air sanctuary of Yazılıkaya, have been on the UNESCO World Heritage List since 1986.

The Treaty of Kadesh, made in the 13th century BC between the Hittite king Hattusili III and Pharaoh Ramesses II of Egypt, is often cited as one of the earliest known written peace treaties; one of its clay-tablet copies is displayed at the Istanbul Archaeology Museums. Before the Hittites, Kültepe (Kanesh) near Kayseri was a trading hub for Assyrian merchants, and its tablets are among the earliest written documents found in Anatolia.

After the Hittite kingdom collapsed around 1200 BC, Neo-Hittite kingdoms rose in the southeast. Karatepe-Aslantaş in Osmaniye, the Yesemek quarry and sculpture workshop in Gaziantep and Arslantepe in Malatya, a UNESCO site since 2021, are key places of this period. The richest Hittite collection is at the Museum of Anatolian Civilizations in Ankara; the Çorum Museum and Boğazköy Museum are also musts.
