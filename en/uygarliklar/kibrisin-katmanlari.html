The layers of Cyprus: from the Bronze Age to the Ottomans

The layers of Cyprus: from the Bronze Age to the Ottomans. In the north of Cyprus, at the crossroads of the eastern Mediterranean, four thousand years of history unfold, from the copper trade to Gothic cathedrals. Thanks to the copper that may have given it its name, the island was a hub of eastern Mediterranean trade in the Bronze Age; Enkomi near Tuzla is a key city of that period. Among the city-kingdoms of the Iron Age, Salamis was the most powerful; the horse and chariot burials of the Royal Tombs, the theatre of Soli and Vouni Palace on its hilltop are traces of this era. The monastery named after the Apostle Barnabas of Salamis and the Ayia Trias Basilica in Karpaz tell of early Christianity. In the Byzantine period the castles of St Hilarion, Buffavento and Kantara were built in the Beşparmak (Kyrenia) mountains. The Lusignan dynasty, ruling from 1192, left the finest Gothic works of the eastern Mediterranean: Bellapais Abbey and the cathedrals of St Sophia in Nicosia (now Selimiye Mosque) and St Nicholas in Famagusta (now Lala Mustafa Pasha Mosque). The Venetians, who took the island in 1489, strengthened Kyrenia Castle and the walls of Famagusta against artillery. With the Ottoman conquest of 1571 the cathedrals became mosques, and buildings such as Büyük Han and the Mevlevi Tekke joined the fabric of Nicosia. The 4th-century-BC shipwreck in Kyrenia Castle symbolises the island's history bound up with the sea.
